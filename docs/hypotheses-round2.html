Round-2 hypotheses — five ideas we had not tried

Round 1 asked "where does the catalogue imply a fault should be?" Round 2 asks "where does the physics say a fault must be, in a place the catalogue never looked?" — which is the population the prize actually scores. Each hypothesis names its layers, its physical signature, why it should catch a fault missing from the USGS/INGENIOUS catalogue rather than one already in it, and how it differs from everything already in this repo. Every geological claim is quoted from a source opened on 2026-09-28; the link is inline. No submission slot was spent before the holdout gate ran.

Why "new fault" includes extensions and splays. DrivenData staff, forum thread 11536 (2026-09-23): "For the purposes of this competition, 'new fault' means 'any fault pixel not already captured by USGS/INGENIOUS' and can include newly mapped geometry of an existing fault system." And thread 11516 (2026-09-16): "Pixels corresponding to known USGS/INGENIOUS faults are masked / excluded from evaluation, so they do not count towards penalty terms." Together these mean the entire score comes from off-catalogue pixels — a continuation past a mapped tip, a splay, or a strand buried under basin fill all count.

N1 · Gravity-gradient edge terminations and intersections

Layers. training_features.tif: isostatic gravity anomaly and the slope of the isostatic gravity anomaly — both enumerated in the official provided-features list. Optional upgrade: INGENIOUS regional gravity/magnetics, DOI 10.5066/P9Z6SA1Z.

Physical signature. A near-vertical density contrast across a fault is a step in the potential field, so |∇g| is a ridge running along the fault — and the ridge vanishes where the contrast dies, i.e. at the fault tip. Two ridges that stop against each other mark an intersection. Code: gems/geoedges.py (ridge_skeleton, edge_terminations, edge_junctions, edge_termination_field).

Why it catches a fault missing from the catalogue. Verbatim from the INGENIOUS structural-settings compilation (Faulds et al., Stanford Geothermal Workshop 2026):

"Isostatic residual and horizontal gradient gravity data were the most useful. For example, terminating and intersecting gravity gradients respectively defined many of the fault terminations and fault intersections. This was especially important in defining FSS in the many basins of the region, where basin-fill sediments obscure the subsurface architecture and primary basin-bounding and/or intrabasinal faults."

The expert inventory itself located fault tips and intersections from gravity gradients, specifically in basins where nothing shows at the surface. A playa floor has no scarp and no lineament — which is exactly why such a fault is absent from a scarp-compiled catalogue — but its density contrast still bends the gravity field. The signal is therefore orthogonal to the catalogue's information source by construction.

How it differs from the repo. H1 computes relay corridors between catalogue tips (it needs the catalogue). H2 runs a generic structure tensor but never extracts gradient ridges, never isolates edge terminations, and never makes the end of an edge the target. H5 uses relief only as a density predictor. Nothing in the repo computes a gravity-gradient ridge, its skeleton, or its endpoints.

N2 · Seismicity-density and strain-invariant lineaments

Layers. Earthquake density; dilatation rate; shear strain rate; second invariant of the strain rate tensor — all in-stack.

Physical signature. Structure-tensor orientation + coherence on the earthquake-density field and on the strain invariant, plus a maximum-filter ridge of the density itself.

Why it catches a missing fault. Same Faulds et al. 2026 passage: basin-fill sediments obscure the subsurface architecture. A fault that has not ruptured in the Holocene and is buried under Lake Lahontan/Bonneville sediments will never be picked from imagery or lidar — so it is not in the catalogue — but it is still slipping and still producing earthquakes. The competition ships the earthquake-density band precisely because INGENIOUS considers it diagnostic.

How it differs. No hypothesis or feature in this repo uses seismicity or kinematics at all. H1–H5 are entirely static structure. This is the only arm whose information source is present-day motion.

N3 · Hydrothermal alteration-cap margin

Layers. Surface conductivity; depth to conductive base surface; reduced-to-pole magnetic anomaly / total magnetic intensity — all in-stack. Optional upgrade: INGENIOUS electrical conductance maps (10.5066/P9TWT2LU) and heat-flow maps (10.5066/P9BZPVUC).

Physical signature. Boolean conjunction cap = (conductance high OR conductive base shallow) AND (magnetic low), then the level-set boundary of that mask, decaying both inward and outward. The fault that feeds a clay cap lies at the cap's margin.

Why it catches a missing fault. Verbatim, Faulds et al. 2026: "magnetic lows and low resistivity anomalies may respectively indicate altered rocks and clay caps at depth induced by geothermal activity." A hydrothermal clay cap is produced by fluid upflow along a fault; the fault must exist at or beside the cap. Alteration is invisible to scarp-based catalogue compilation, so this is a target class the catalogue cannot produce by construction. This is the most contrarian of the five: it targets the geothermal signal rather than the structural one.

How it differs. H3 uses point features (springs, sinter/tufa, vents) from the INGENIOUS release — a surface-expression approach needing new external data. N3 is a 2-D mask-margin transform on bands already in the stack, and its physical target (clay cap) is a different object from a spring.

N4 · 1 m lidar scarp lineaments + paleo-shoreline negative mask

Layers. 1 m lidar DEM from the USGS 3D Elevation Program (3DEP), via the 1m_DEM_links.csv the sponsor ships on the data tab, or directly from 3DEP. Fallback: in-stack detrended elevation and its slope at 100 m.

Physical signature. Multi-azimuth hillshade + sky-view-factor / local relief model, then a profile-curvature (second derivative along the slope-normal) ridge detector — the transform that isolates a scarp's break of slope from a general slope. Plus a negative mask: long, quasi-straight, low-curvature benches following ancient lake shorelines are removed.

Why it catches a missing fault. Two verified statements.

  1. The sponsor's own About page records that airborne lidar was collected through 3DEP "over a similar extent spanned by the geophysical surveys". At 1 m, scarps invisible at the 100 m competition resolution become detectable.
  2. Hermant et al., SGW 2025 (cited by the sponsor) documents, for the same region: USGS Qfault positions differing from lidar-derived ground truth by up to 400 m; new faults detected by their CNN and confirmed by experts on lidar; and that both of their models also detect paleo-shorelines as false positives.

The 400 m figure matters directly: the competition's kernel radius is 300 m, and the problem description itself says "portions of the existing fault data may be misaligned from the true location of the surface fault, which is the prediction target". Lidar lets us put the prediction on the true trace rather than the catalogue's displaced one.

Why the shoreline mask is the key idea. Faulds et al. 2026: "much of the GBR was inundated by late Pleistocene lakes, and thus faults that have not ruptured in the Holocene are obscured by lake sediments and shoreline features." Lake shorelines are long, straight and scarp-like — the single largest false-positive class for any topographic lineament detector in this region.

How it differs. No DEM-based scarp or curvature feature exists anywhere in this repo. H5 uses relief only as a density predictor, never as a detector, and there is no negative-evidence / FP-suppression mask of any kind in the repo.

N5 · Range-front topographic step and interbasinal high

Layers. Detrended elevation and the slope of detrended elevation — in-stack.

Physical signature. Slope magnitude + structure-tensor coherence of the detrended elevation, plus a distance transform to the top-decile slope (the "front"). A range front is a long, coherent, high-slope lineation; a step along it is the step-over proxy; an interbasinal high is a locally elevated block between oppositely dipping systems.

Why it catches a missing fault. Verbatim, Faulds et al. 2026: "Topographic steps along the fronts of mountain ranges/fault blocks may delineate step-overs or relay ramps. Interbasinal highs commonly correspond to accommodation zones between oppositely dipping Quaternary fault systems." The same paper reports that fault terminations, intersections, step-overs and accommodation zones are "characterized by closely-spaced, relatively minor faults with minimal recent surface ruptures" — precisely the structures a regional catalogue omits. Quantitative prior from the same source: step-overs occupy 33.3 % of all FSS area, terminations 20.3 %, accommodation zones 19.3 %, intersections 17.4 %, median FSS polygon ~13.4 km².

How it differs. H5's completeness residual compares catalogue density with strain/relief expectations — a regional density statistic. This is a local geometric detector on the range front with no catalogue input. H1's relay corridors are built from catalogue tip pairs; these are built from the topographic front.

What the blocked holdout decided

Protocol: 4 spatial folds of 48-px blocks with a 3-px purge buffer; hide-and-recover logistic regression with a fresh random component-hiding plan every epoch and the anti-leak assertion run every epoch; identical model, seeds and protocol across arms; three region seeds. Two targets, because staff declined to disclose the provenance of the hidden test faults (forum 11527):

  • recovery — catalogue traces held out of the visible context (the standard hide-and-recover proxy);
  • discovery — faults present in the synthetic geophysics but absent from the catalogue by construction (≥ 22 px from every catalogue trace) and never used as training labels — the local analogue of the private test set;
  • combined — the union, the best available guess at the population the prize scores.

The three findings that change the strategy

  1. The literature-ranked top candidate was killed by the gate — and that is the gate working. N1 had the strongest published support (a verbatim statement from the INGENIOUS authors) and the lowest implementation cost, and as a classifier feature it made the blocked holdout slightly worse. Its information is real — the raw n1_term_field alone scores DTI 0.08–0.15 against blind faults, ~20× the catalogue-distance feature — but feeding it to a model trained on catalogue labels dilutes the catalogue signal. No submission slot was spent on it.
  2. The multiplicative blend is the winner, and it wins on both populations. It improves recovery (0.3073 → 0.3673) and discovery (0.0081 → 0.0612, 7.5×). The additive and max blends both destroy the classifier's localisation: they smear probability mass over the prior's support, which costs FP everywhere without lifting the per-ground-truth-pixel maximum. The multiplicative form keeps the classifier's thin traces and only boosts them where the geophysics independently agrees.
  3. A catalogue-trained model is structurally bad at discovery — and discovery is the entire competition. The baseline's discovery DTI is 0.0081 because it has never been shown a fault absent from the catalogue. Since the prize masks known-fault pixels and scores only off-catalogue faults, the baseline is optimising a population that contributes nothing to the score.

Promotion decision

  • Slot-eligible (pending the real-raster re-run): BLEND-MUL, then N2, then N5.
  • Killed by the holdout gate: N1 (as a classifier feature), N3, GEO-ONLY, BLEND, BLEND-ADD.
  • Not yet viable: N4 — needs a verified free official paleo-shoreline source (FLAG #1b).
